# 1.3 — Evolution of Microservices Architecture: How We Got Here

> Section 1: Introduction to Microservices Architecture

Let me start our microservices journey by answering the most basic question, what actually is a microservice? In simple language, a microservice is nothing but one style of architecture, one way of designing and building our web applications. But a definition on its own won't stick, so instead of memorizing a sentence, let's try to understand why this architecture even exists. And to do that, we had to travel back in time and watch how the industry struggled and how each new architecture was born to solve the pain of the previous one.

Let me give a running example that we will use for the entire course, a bank application called EazyBank. Just like any real bank, EazyBank offers three products to its customers: accounts, cards, and loans. Keep these three in your head because we will build and rebuild the same bank three different ways. First, let's start with the old way. A few decades back, if you want to build EazyBank application, there was really one popular choice, which is monolith.

In a monolithic application, all the business functionality is packaged and deployed as a single unit. Picture one server, inside it, you see your presentation layer, which will have HTML, CSS, JavaScript, then your backend business logic for accounts, cards, and loans, then your data access layer. And behind all of it, a single database storing every bit of data your bank owns. Think of it like one giant building where every department shares the same walls, the same doors, and the same foundation.

That design has real strengths and real problems. First, let's try to understand why teams loved these monolith. For a small team or a small application, a monolith is beautifully simple. One code base, one deployment, need to deploy once, and your entire application is live for your users. Your cross-cutting concerns like security, logging, auditing are easier too because everything sits inside one server. So there is nothing to wire across a network.

And performance is excellent. When your account logic needs something from loans, it's just a method call inside the same server. No network hub, no web service, no latency. Now, let's try to understand why teams eventually hated this architecture. Let's imagine EazyBank application grows, the accounts team reads about a shiny new framework and wants to upgrade, but they can't because the cards and loans team share the same code base and push back.

So the whole bank stays stuck on old technology. Adopting anything new means touching the entire code base and running rigorous regression testing across features you never even changed because everything is tightly coupled and nobody is sure what will break. And here is that disadvantage that hurts the most, suppose the loans team wants to ship one tiny enhancement because it's all one deployable unit, that one change forces a full redeployment and your entire bank goes down during it.

Accounts and cards has nothing to do with these new change, but their customers feel the downtime too. For an application, people uses 24/7, that's a serious problem, right? The monolith also can't fail gracefully. If one part falls over, it can take the whole application with it. So scalability and availability become constant headaches. To better understanding more details about this monolith, let's picture the teams: accounts, cards, loans, and UI/UX developers, all working on one shared code base, packaging everything into your WAR or EAR file, and deploying it as one application backed by one database through a CI/CD tool like Jenkins or GitHub Actions.

With this setup, the organization has almost zero freedom to move fast. And as customer demands kept rising, the industry went looking for something better. That something better was service-oriented architecture. SOA introduce one big idea, stop mixing your UI and your backend. In SOA, we split them. The presentation layer goes onto Server One and the backend logic accounts, cards, loans goes onto Server Two, exposed as web services. Right away, we have broken the tight coupling between the front end and the backend.

That alone was a big win. But there is a catch, for the UI to talk to the backend, SOA puts a middleware component in the middle called the enterprise service bus. Every request from the UI hits the ESB first and the ESB decides where to route it, whether it has to go to accounts, cards, or loans, and forward it to the right service on Server Two. Let's try to understand the advantages of SOA. Inside SOA, since the logic is now split into separate components like a dedicated account service, a card service, a loan service, and a separate UI code base on its own server, the development teams, they got real benefits around reusability of services, better maintainability, higher reliability, and the ability to develop in parallel.

Coming to the disadvantages, the communication typically inside SOA flowed over heavy protocols like SOAP over XML, which is complex and bulky compared to lighter options like REST. And that ESB, in the middle, was not free. These are commercial products like Oracle ESB, expensive to license and maintain. So now you are paying a lot of money and adding an extra hub between your UI and your services just to let them talk to each other. Ask yourself, "Why should I maintain a whole separate product "just for communication?"

Early projects adopted SOA and genuinely got some of those benefits like parallel development, reuse, better maintainability. But over the time, the industry realized SOA was still complex to run and carried a heavy investment cost. Let's picture SOA the same way that we pictured monolith. The UI/UX team has its own repo. All the backend services shared a backend repo, two separate deployments onto two separate servers. This is real progress over the monolith, but every conversation between the UI server and the backend server still has to pass through that one middle component, the ESB.

Good separation, but still there are some constraints. That's why that search for something better is exactly where microservices were born. So let's build EazyBank one more time, this time with the microservices. The core idea is we build many small services and each one is modeled around a single business domain. One microservices focused entirely on accounts, another entirely on cards, another entirely on loans. Each of these microservices gets its own database and each is deployed into its own server or Docker container.

We're going to talk about Docker and the containers a lot in the coming sections. If the monolith was one giant building, microservices are a food court, a row of independent stalls. Each stall run its own kitchen, its own menu, its own staff. If one stall closes for the night, the others keep serving. That independence is the whole point. So now the UI logic lives on its own. And on the backend, we can spin up as many microservices as we need, each with its own supporting database.

Why microservices is so powerful? Everything is loosely coupled. Your account logic lives in its own small component with its own development and deployment cycle. If you want to rewrite the account service in a new framework, go ahead, it won't touch cards or loans. With no tight coupling, every team run its own enhancement, its own discussions, its own release schedule. Team stops waiting on each other. Usually inside microservices, all the services are modeled around the business domains.

For example, in our bank scenario, we have three different services like accounts, cards, and loans. That's why we can say these microservices architecture mirrors how the business actually thinks. Because these microservices run in Docker containers, you can scale a single service on its own. If accounts get hammered with the traffic while cards and loans stay quiet, you scale only accounts. You don't waste the resources scaling the whole application.

Now, a very important warning. Microservices are not a silver bullet. Please don't jump into microservices for every project. For a small team or a small application, they are overkill. So let's be honest about the disadvantages of microservices. The first one is complexity. Once you have dozens of independent containers spread across servers and clusters, getting them to find and talk to each other reliably becomes genuinely hard. Next, we have infrastructure overhead.

In a monolith or SOA, you monitor one or two servers. Whereas in microservices, you are monitoring many servers and containers. That's a lot more to watch. Next we have security concerns. In a monolith, internal communication was just in-process method calls. Now with microservices, your backend services talk to each other over the network using REST-based calls. And every one of those network calls is a new surface you have to secure. Don't let that discourage you.

At the end of the day, organizations want agility and the freedom to shape whenever they want and reach the market fast. Microservices deliver exactly that. So companies happily take on these challenges, and every one of these challenges has proven solutions, which we will work throughout together across this course. If you have to take away one crucial idea from this entire course, let it be this: Microservices practice the independent deployability of your services.

When you deploy your microservice, you have zero dependency on any other microservice. Nail that one principle, and almost every other benefit of microservices emerges naturally from it. We will see how step-by-step in the coming sections. Let's close by picturing microservices the way we did the others. This time also, there are four teams: accounts, cards, loans, and UI/UX. Each has its own deployment lifecycle through Jenkins or any CI/CD tool and gets deployed on its own server or containers, each with its own database.

And here is a freedom that gets people excited. The accounts team can use a SQL database, while loans uses a NoSQL database. Accounts can be built in Python, loans in Java, cards and Go. Every team picks a language, framework, and database that fits their problem best. No shared code base, no shared handcuffs. This is the real superpower microservices hand to your company and your developers. So there we have it, monolith, SOA, and microservices with the advantages and disadvantages of each and the story of why the industry moved from one to the next.

Make sure this progression is clear in your mind because everything we built from here rests on it. We will continue our microservices discussion in the next lecture. Thank you, and I'll catch you there.
