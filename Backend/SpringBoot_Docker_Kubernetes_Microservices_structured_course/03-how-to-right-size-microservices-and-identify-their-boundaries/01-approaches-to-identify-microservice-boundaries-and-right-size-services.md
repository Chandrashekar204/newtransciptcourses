# 3.1 — Approaches to Identify Microservice Boundaries & Right-Size Services

> Section 3: How to Right-Size Microservices & Identify Their Boundaries

Hello, everyone. Welcome to your brand new section. First of all, congratulations. So far, you have successfully built three production style microservices. That's a great achievement, but here is something interesting. Building Rest APIs is actually not the hardest part of building a microservice architecture. The real challenge starts now. In this section, we are going to discuss one of the most important architectural decisions you will ever make while building microservices.

In our previous section, we solved challenge one, which is how do we build microservices using Rest APIs? Now it's time for challenge two. The challenge is how do we decide what should actually becomes a microservice? In other words, how do we identify the right service boundaries? This sounds simple, but it's one of the biggest reasons why many microservice projects succeed or fail. Let me explain this using something we all use every day. Imagine you are buying a T-shirt, you have different sizes.

Would someone who normally wears a medium choose a double XL? Of course not. It would be too loose and uncomfortable. Similarly, someone who wears an XL T-shirt cannot comfortably wear a small T-shirt. The lesson is simple. Choosing the right size matters. Interestingly, Agile teams or scrum teams use the same concept while estimating user stories. We often classify stories as small, medium, large, or XL based on complexity. Microservices follow a very similar principle.

You may have a question here. Why rightsizing matters? Let me answer the same. A microservice should never be too large. If it becomes too large, it starts behaving like a mini-monolith. You lose many of the benefits of microservices such as independent deployment and easier maintenance. On the other hand, a microservice should never be too small either. Imagine having hundreds of tiny services. Each service needs deployment, monitoring, logging, configuration, security, networking, and communication with the other services.

Instead of solving problems, you have now created an operational nightmare. So the goal is to find the right balance. Not too big, not too small. Just the right size for the business capability it represents. So how do we find the right size? That's a obvious next question. How do organizations decide the size of a microservice? There are many approaches, but two of the most commonly used ones are domain-driven sizing and even storming sizing. Domain-driven sizing is probably the most popular approach.

The basic idea is simple. Every organization has different business domains. For example, in our EazyBank application, we have domains like accounts, loans, cards. Each of these domains represents a separate business capability. That's exactly why we built three different microservices. Simple enough, right? But real world applications are rarely this straightforward. Imagine a very large banking organization. The loans department alone may have hundreds of developers working on dozens of different products.

In search of situations, creating one huge loans microservice may not be the right choice. So what do organizations do? They involve domain experts, architects, business analysts, product owners, developers, clients, and anyone who understands the business. Together, they study questions like what possibilities does this domain have? Which teams work on it? Which business capabilities change independently? Which operations naturally belong together?

These discussions usually don't finish in a day. In large organizations, they can take several months. Initially, the team proposes a service boundary based on assumptions. Later after development begins, they continuously refine it. Sometimes they split a large microservice into smaller ones. Sometimes they merge multiple small services into one. So remember this important point, rightsizing is not a one-time activity. It's an ongoing process. This domain-driven sizing, it has limitations.

Although it works extremely well, it depends heavily on people who deeply understands the business. Finding all those experts and collecting their knowledge can take significant time. That's why another popular approach exists. It starts with the business events. Imagine 20 stakeholders standing together in a meeting room. There are developers, testers, architects, business analysts, product owners, clients. Everyone receives a stack of sticky notes.

Now, the facilitator asks a simple question. What events can happen inside this application? People begin writing events like customer created, payment completed, loan approved, card issued, money transferred. Every possible business event goes onto a sticky note. Once all the events are identified, the next step is to ask what command triggers this event? For example, a customer clicks the Pay Now button, that command triggers the payment completed event.

After every event, another important question follows, what happens next? These are called reactions. For example, deduct to money from the account, update the transaction history, send a notification. Interestingly, one reaction can become the command for another event. That's why event storming naturally reveals the complete business workflow. Finally, all these events are grouped based upon their business domains. For example, accounts, loans, cards, payments.

Once these groups become clear, identifying microservice boundaries becomes much easier. Compared to domain-driven sizing, event storming has several advantages. It is highly interactive, collaborative, fast, visual, easy for both the technical and non-technical stakeholders. Even people who are not domain experts can contribute because they are simply describing the events they observe while using the system. That's why many organizations today use event storming workshops before designing their microservice architecture.

I have also included a blog from Lucid Chat that explains how event storming workshops are conducted in real organizations. Even if you never become a facilitator USL, it's worth spending 10 minutes reading through it. In interviews, it's quite common to be asked, how would you identify the boundaries of a microservice, understanding that event storming can help you answer that question with the confidence? I'll also add this reference to the GitHub Read Me so you can explore it later.

Let me quickly show you what exactly we have inside this blog. So first they explained what is event storming, what are its advantages, followed by steps of event storming. First, we need to invite the right people, provide unlimited modeling space. These are typically color-coded sticky notes. As part of step three, explore the business domain. Again, this step three has many sub-steps like identifying domain events, followed by connecting domain events to commands, next to connecting events to reactions.

Once we have events, reacts and commands towards the end, the domain boundaries or the microservice boundaries can be identified. To summarize, whenever you are designing microservices, the first question should never be how many microservices do we need? Instead, ask yourself what is the right boundary for each business capability? Choosing the right service boundaries is one of the most important architectural decisions in any microservice project.

In this lecture, we explored two widely used approaches, domain-driven sizing and event storming. In the next lecture, we will apply these ideas to our EazyBank application and see how we arrived at the accounts, loans, and cards microservice. That practical example will make these concepts much easier to understand. So I'll see you in the next lecture, bye!
