# 3.2 — How Small Is Too Small? Right-Sizing a Banking App

> Section 3: How to Right-Size Microservices & Identify Their Boundaries

In the previous lecture, we discussed two popular approaches for identifying the right size and service boundaries of microservices. We learned that designing microservices is not just about writing code, it's about making the right architectural decisions. Now, let's make this discussion much more practical. Imagine a bank that has been running a large monolith application for several years. The management has finally decided to modernize the system and migrate to a microservices architecture.

Before writing even a single line of code, the leadership felt that one very important question needs to be answered. How many microservices should we build and what should each one be responsible for? This is exactly the challenge we are going to solve in this lecture. Let's make the scenario even more interesting. Suppose the CEO or the CTO of the bank creates three independent architecture teams. Each team is asked to study the business and propose the best service boundaries.

They may use domain-driven design, event storming, or any other architectural technique. Let's look what each team proposes. The first team believes the application can be divided into just two microservices. The first microservice contains both saving account, trading account. The second microservice contains cards and loans. Their argument is straightforward. These business capabilities are closely related, so grouping them together should reduce the number of services and simplify the architecture.

At first glance, this sounds like a reasonable idea, but is it really? Let's keep that thought for a moment and see what the other teams suggest. The second team takes a different approach. Instead of combining business domains, they recommended creating one microservice for each major business capability. They propose four independent microservices covering saving account, trading account, cards, loans. Each one wants its own business logic and can evolve independently.

The third team goes one step further. They believe even these services are too large. Instead of single card service, they suggest separate services for debit card and credit card. Similarly, instead of one loan service, they proposed three different services covering home, vehicle, and personal loan. In total, the application is now divided into several very small microservices. Time to become the CTO. Now it's your turn. Imagine you are the chief technology officer of this bank.

Which proposal would you approve? Let's evaluate each option. The first option creates only two microservices. Although this reduces the number of services, it introduces another problem. Saving account and trading accounts become tightly coupled. Similarly, cards and loans also become tightly coupled. So imagine the cards team wants to release a new feature. Since cards and loans are part of the same microservice, the loans team may also be affected.

This reduces the deployment, independence, and the increases coordination between teams. For these reasons, I don't think this is the ideal design. Now, let's evaluate the second proposal. Personally, I believe this is the best choice for our current application. Why, because every major business capabilities has its own dedicated microservice. This uses several advantages. Each team can develop independently, deploy independently, choose its own technology stack if required, and evolve without impacting unrelated business domains.

This is exactly the kind of loose coupling we usually aim for in a microservice architecture. You may have a question, then, in our previous section, we only built accounts microservice, but not saving account and trading account. Here, I have taken these examples just to explain about right sizing in more effective manner, but for our microservices discussions, we will go with the three microservices because building four or five or many more microservices is not going to make sense as the topics will be duplicated again and again.

What about this third proposal? Is it wrong? Not at all. In fact, it may be the right solution for some organizations, but it depends entirely on the business. Suppose that debit card and credit card products have completely different business rules, separate development teams, different release cycles, and independent scaling requirements. In that case, splitting them into separate microservices could make perfect sense. The same applies to home loans, vehicle loans, and personal loans.

However, in our current banking application, these products share most of their business logic. The difference are relatively small. Creating separate microservices for each product would introduce unnecessary operational complexity. We would have more deployments, more monitoring, more networking, more configuration management, and more maintenance. That's a lot of overhead for very little benefit. If I were the CTO of this bank today, I would choose Team 2's proposal.

It provides a good balance. The services are neither too large, not too small. More importantly, they align nicely with the major business capabilities of the organization. Now, I would like you to remember one important point. There is no such thing as a perfect microservice design on day one. Microservice architecture is not something you design once and never touch again. As new products are introduced, as traffic patterns change, your microservice boundaries may also need to evolve.

Sometimes you will split a large service into smaller ones. Sometimes you will merge multiple small services into your large one. Both are perfectly normal. Architecture is a continuous journey of learning and refinement. So what did we learn in this lecture? We evaluated three different approaches for designing the same banking application. Instead of asking which design is correct, we asked a much better question. Which design best fits our current business needs?

For our banking application, team two provides the most balanced solution. But remember, tomorrow the business may evolve and our architecture should be flexible enough to evolve with it. In the next lecture, we will continue building on these architectural concepts and explore the next important challenge in designing successful microservice systems. Thank you, and I will see you in the next lecture.
