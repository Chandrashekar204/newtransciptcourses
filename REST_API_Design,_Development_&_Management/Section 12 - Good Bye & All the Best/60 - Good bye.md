# Good bye

*Section: Good Bye & All the Best*

This is the last lecture in this course. I'll go over the topics that we have covered and some of the key highlights. In the first section, I covered the foundational concepts of APIs, the rest API constraints and the Richardson maturity model. Your services can be called Rest APIs if and only if they follow the restful API constraints that we discussed in this section. In the second section we looked at how many of these organizations are designing their APIs.

We discussed the options for building features like pagination, partial responses, error handling, etcetera. I also discuss some best practices that you can use for designing of your own APIs. The best way to understand the options and practices that are discussed in this section is to practice and build some APIs. My suggestion is that at this point you build some cool APIs and share it with your peers.

I'll be more than happy to look at your APIs and share with you my $0.02. Third section was on security. With an API, you're providing controlled access to the enterprise resources. But unfortunately, with the APIs sitting on the edge of the enterprise, you are creating an attack surface for the hackers who can exploit the vulnerabilities in your API. Suggestion is to test your thoroughly for vulnerabilities.

The two most important takeaways from this section are that functional attacks are real and you cannot ignore those. And you must use standard security schemes for all APIs. That is, irrespective of whether your API is an internal API, a public API or a partner API. Use a standard scheme. Look at how popular providers are securing their APIs and keep yourself updated on new type of attacks that may be launched against your APIs.

In the section on Swagger, you learn how to develop swagger specifications for Rest APIs. Swagger 2.0 or specification have become a de facto standard. It has a very vibrant community and vendor support. I suggest that you adopt Swagger for your rest APIs. Design your APIs using swagger or specification by collaborating with the app developer. You have to keep in mind that app developers are the most important stakeholder in the value chain.

For high level of adoption for your API, you must fulfill the needs of the app developer. Suggestion is to adopt a collaborative and iterative contract first process. A well designed and well thought out API can help the organization meet its goals. Goals may be direct or indirect revenue generation or brand recognition. At the same time, if the API is not secure or managed poorly, it may negatively impact the organization's reputation and may directly or indirectly impact the revenue.

To ensure that your APIs lead to a desired result for your organization, you need to manage its lifecycle like any other product your organization produces, including managing it at runtime to meet the SLA that you have promised to your consumers. In the section on API management, I covered the activities such as developer productivity lifecycle management, analytics, traffic management and other aspects.

All these tasks can be easily handled with the API management products which are available in the market today. I used three products in my lectures as examples. The IBM API Connect Apigee and MuleSoft. But there are multiple other products which are available in the market. You can use the trial version of these products to understand what they offer in terms of features. There's a lot of buzz around economy and monetization.
